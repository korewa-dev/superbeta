The Rise and Decline of State Religions

How rulers have used religion to legitimise power, and why modern states are slowly moving away from established faiths.

Religion and power have always made an uneasy bargain. Rulers want legitimacy, and priests want protection. Across history, the result has been the state religion: a faith imposed from above, financed by taxes, and used to bless conquests, crown kings, and silence critics. The bargain works well for both parties and poorly for everyone else.

A Royal Asset

Roman emperors adopted Christianity, Persian kings their own creed, and Muslim and European rulers alike built empires on divine mandate. Being God's chosen made rebellion a sin as well as a crime. It is a remarkably efficient form of control.

The Long Decline

The hold has weakened. War between competing religions, the growth of trade and science, and the spread of secular ideas made official religion a liability. Many states have formally separated church and state, even where populations remain devout.

The pattern is worth noticing. When religion serves the state, the religion tends to mirror the state's ambitions. When it is free, it is freer to speak truth to power.
