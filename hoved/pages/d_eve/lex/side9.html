Religious Nationalism and Modern Democracy

How religious nationalism reconciles with democratic procedure, and why majoritarian rule does not equal democratic protection.

Religious nationalism merges faith with the state's claim to legitimacy, arguing that the nation's identity is defined by a particular religion. It is often democratic in form, winning votes and controlling parliaments, yet it strains democracy's deeper commitments.

Majority Rule Is Not Democracy

Democracy is more than counting votes; it is a system of guaranteed rights that majorities cannot extinguish. Religious nationalists frequently treat the majority's faith as the source of law, which reduces minorities and non-believers to tolerated guests rather than equal citizens. Where personal status, education, and media are controlled by religious doctrine, formal elections coexist with shrinking freedom.

The Modern Tension

Globalization intensifies the appeal of religious nationalism as a source of identity and certainty in a changing world. Yet the same openness that feeds it also exposes religious minorities, secularists, and reformers to increasing pressure.

The question modern democracies must answer is whether the majority may rewrite the terms of belonging. A society that ties citizenship to creed can remain majoritarian indefinitely while violating the equality that makes democracy meaningful. Religious nationalism and durable democratic rights ultimately pull in opposite directions.
