National Identity and Dominant Religions

How majority religions become entangled with national identity, and the exclusionary consequences for minorities.

National identity is a story a country tells about itself. When that story is fused with a dominant religion, belonging to the nation becomes bound up with belonging to the faith, and citizenship risks being treated as a privilege of the majority rather than a right of all.

The Fusion of Faith and Nation

Religious nationalism often emerges during periods of colonization, political transition, or external threat, when faith supplies a sense of cohesion. Symbols, holidays, and language become saturated with religious meaning. In Bangladesh, Pakistan, and India alike, contests over how much religion defines the nation have shaped constitutions and political conflict.

The Cost of Exclusion

When identity is religious, minorities are made to prove their loyalty repeatedly. They may be pressured to celebrate majority holidays, accept religious law, or face suspicion as outsiders. The nation becomes a hierarchy rather than a shared political space.

A healthier national identity is civic: defined by constitution, rights, and participation rather than creed. Such an identity allows diverse citizens to belong fully, strengthening the nation by including everyone who lives within it.
