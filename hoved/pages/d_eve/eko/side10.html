Prosperity Theology as an Economic Phenomenon

How the teaching that faith brings wealth redistributes savings from the poor to church leaders.

Prosperity theology — the teaching that faith brings wealth, health, and success — has become a global industry. Preached across continents, it turns worship into an investment strategy.

The Core Exchange

The message is simple: give to God, and God will return more. Donations, called seed offerings or sowing, are presented as transactions with a guaranteed spiritual return. Believers are taught that poverty is a lack of faith, so the poor — who can least afford to give — are pushed to give most. Pastors model the pitch with private jets, which believers are told reflect the blessing they too can receive.

Who Wins and Loses

The economics are asymmetric. Preachers become wealthy on small, devoted donations, while the poor lose savings they cannot spare. Wealth that could start a small business or pay school fees is transferred to a few church leaders. There is no evidence that sowing increases income; there is abundant evidence that it increases church income.

A Rational Reading

Prosperity theology succeeds because it speaks to genuine hope. A secular critique need not mock that hope — it can point out that giving to your own education and community offers a more reliable return than any offering plate.
