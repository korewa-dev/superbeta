Religious Institutions as Economic Actors

How temples, churches, and mosques operate as major employers, landowners, and investors — with real obligations.

Religious institutions are not only places of worship; they are businesses in their own right. They employ staff, own land, run schools and hospitals, manage investments, and trade in goods from candles to gold.

Real Economic Power

Temple trusts in India administer wealth comparable to corporations, funding festivals and real estate. The Catholic Church operates one of the world's largest school and hospital networks. Mosques and madrasas in Bangladesh employ thousands of teachers and administrators, while waqf estates hold valuable urban property. Like any large organization, these institutions generate income and pay salaries.

What That Means

Being an economic actor brings obligations. Institutions that employ workers should follow labor law; owners of property should pay their share of taxes; managers of donations owe their donors honest accounting. When religious bodies claim exemption from these duties because they are spiritual, they ask for a privilege no other organization enjoys.

A Level Playing Field

None of this requires hostility to religion. A secular state simply insists that the same rules apply to all: fair wages, transparent books, and equal treatment before the law. Faith can inspire organizations, but it should not exempt them from being good citizens of the marketplace.
