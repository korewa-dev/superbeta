Tax Exemptions for Religious Organizations

Why religious tax privileges raise questions of fairness, accountability, and state neutrality.

Churches, temples, and mosques enjoy tax exemptions in nearly every country. What began as a way to protect worship now raises uncomfortable questions about fairness and accountability.

A Growing Privilege

Religious organizations often pay no income, property, or sales tax, even when they run businesses. Religious property is often exempt even when used for commercial rental. In many countries, madrasas and temple trusts control land worth fortunes while contributing little to public coffers. Schools, hospitals, and charitable arms of faith groups can out-compete secular providers precisely because they do not pay taxes.

The Opportunity Cost

Every exemption is a subsidy funded by other taxpayers. Money not collected from wealthy religious institutions must be raised elsewhere or cut from services. When faith groups use tax-free money to lobby, the arrangement moves from religious freedom toward state support for religion.

Seeking Balance

Most societies agree that genuine worship should not be taxed. The difficulty lies in drawing the line between worship and commerce. Transparent rules that tax business income, while protecting actual religious practice, would treat believers and nonbelievers alike and keep the state neutral.
