milkmagic

The 1995 Ganesha milk miracle as a case study in how superstition can move markets, prices, and temple revenues overnight.

On 21 September 1995, millions of Hindus across India — and soon around the world — were reported to believe that statues of the god Ganesha were drinking milk offered by worshippers. People queued for hours with spoons full of milk, and temples were overwhelmed. Within days the phenomenon ended as abruptly as it began.

The Science and the Spread

The simple explanation is capillary action: milk seeped into the porous stone and ceramic surfaces of the statues and pooled where the spoon rested, appearing to be drunk. The real mystery is how quickly an unverified claim traveled through newspapers, television, and word of mouth, emptying shops of milk and boosting temple revenues.

An Economic Mirror

The episode is a miniature case study in superstition economics. Devotees spent heavily, dairy prices spiked, and temple treasuries swelled — all on a physical illusion. The lesson for critical thinking is direct: extraordinary beliefs can have very ordinary, very financial, causes and consequences. Testing claims is cheaper than the rituals built on them.
