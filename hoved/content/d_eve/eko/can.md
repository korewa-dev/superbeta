---
title: Can Secular Governance Improve Economic Efficiency?
description: Why a state that favors no religion lowers transaction costs, builds trust, and frees capital for production.
---

Secular governance — a state that treats all religions equally and favors none — is often defended on moral grounds. It may also be good economics.

## Removing Friction

When religion and state are entangled, markets pay costs. Legal systems built on religious law can be unpredictable for minorities; clerical approval can become a license to operate; tax exemptions and privileges for favored groups distort competition. A secular state simplifies the rules: one law, applied equally, understood by everyone. Businesses save the time and money spent on religious navigation and favor-seeking.

## Trust as an Asset

Equal treatment builds the kind of trust that lowers transaction costs. When citizens believe courts, banks, and police serve everyone regardless of faith, they invest more, trade more, and cooperate more. India and France, despite very different histories, both show that religious-neutral legal frameworks attract investment and talent.

## No Faith Needed

Secularism asks no one to abandon belief. It simply moves religious matters to the private sphere, where the state neither subsidizes nor punishes them. For economies, that is a practical bargain: less money spent on religion's entanglement with power, and more spent on production. Efficiency is not secularism's only argument — but it is a strong one.
